The Tech Co-op Playbook

A Mad Libs‑style resume framework. Pick a template, grab bullets for your courses, replace the [brackets], and ship your application. Also covers the UofR co-op match process, how to answer behavioral and technical questions, and AI prompts that actually help.

⚠  Do not submit a resume that still contains [brackets]. Every bracket is a variable you must replace with your own data.

Step 1

Pick Your Template

Both templates are based on Jake Gutierrez’s resume template (MIT License): single-column, ATS-friendly, and used by engineers at top companies. Open the template in Overleaf, fill in your information, and compile.

From the Field On GPA: The co-op eligibility floor is 70%. That's a minimum to apply, not a target to aim for. Recruiters see your transcript, and a borderline GPA means everything else - projects, bullets, interview - has to work harder to compensate. Aim higher, not because of a magic number, but because the habits that produce a strong GPA are the same habits that make a good co-op student: staying current, not falling behind, asking for help early.
From the Field On resume structure: The templates here put Education first, then Experience, then Skills. That ordering is intentional. Recruiters reading a student resume want to know your degree, GPA, and graduation date immediately, then understand what you’ve done. A summary section is optional - skip it unless you have something genuinely specific to say. A generic summary takes up half an inch and adds nothing.
From the Field On side projects: A to-do app or basic CRUD website belongs in your learning, not on your resume. Recruiters have seen hundreds of them. What stands out is a project built under real constraints - a hackathon deadline, a genuine problem you wanted to solve, actual users, something technically non-obvious. Attend hackathons. Build things that give you a real story to tell in an interview, not checkboxes to fill on a resume.

Step 2

Bullet Library

Find your course, pick an angle, click Copy to grab a bullet. Replace every [bracket] with your specific project name, tool, and metric.

Course syllabi change over time. Bullets are templates built around common course frameworks; adapt them to match what you actually built in your specific section of the course.

Course not listed? Write your own.

Every good bullet in this library follows the same structure: Action verb → what you built or did → technology or tool → result or outcome. Apply that to any course project.

1
Start with what you actually built or implemented — not what the course covered in theory. If you implemented a scheduler, wrote a parser, trained a model, built a UI — that is your action.
2
Name the specific language, tool, or framework you used. “Python” is better than nothing. “Python (scikit-learn, Pandas)” is better than “Python.”
3
End with a result. What did it produce, enable, or demonstrate? Quantify if you can. If you can’t, describe the outcome: “enabling real-time queries on [X] records” or “reducing manual steps from [N] to one.”
Weak “Learned about operating system concepts including scheduling and memory management.”
Strong “Implemented a Round Robin CPU scheduler in C, simulating process queuing for [N] concurrent processes and analyzing turnaround time vs. quantum size trade-offs.”

AI Tools

AI Prompts

Stop asking ChatGPT to “write your resume.” That produces generic text recruiters hate. Use these prompts as a starting point, then rewrite the output in your own voice.

Important: Always review AI-generated output before using it. Never copy-paste directly. Recruiters and hiring managers can spot AI-polished language instantly, and so can the people reading your LinkedIn messages. Use these prompts to get a rough draft, then rewrite it in your own natural language, add your own personality, and cut anything that sounds like it came from a robot. The goal is a message that sounds like you, not a template.
